Study Fuel: a full accounting cycle

I set up a simulated company that sells energy drinks to students, ran it for four weeks, and kept its books from the first purchase to the closing entries.

The brief

Start a business with $100,000 of capital, record every transaction for four weeks, and produce a complete set of financial statements. Daily sales were tied to the real daily price move of a listed stock, so revenue changed every day and could not be planned in advance.

What I built

  • Startup cost list with a source link for every purchase: storage shed, delivery van, coolers, vending machine and supplies.
  • Journal entries for each week, including cash and credit sales, cost of goods sold calculated per can, a one-year loan at 7%, a bond purchase and a stock investment.
  • Adjusting entries for accrued interest, prepaid insurance and advertising, supplies used and the market value of the investment.
  • Depreciation schedules using straight-line for the building and trademark and double-declining balance for equipment.
  • General ledger, trial balances and closing entries, with a separate receivable account for each day of credit sales and bad debts handled by direct write-off.
  • Financial statements: income statement, statement of comprehensive income and balance sheet.

What it shows

Every number on the statements traces back through the ledger to a dated journal entry, and every formula is live, so changing one sale flows through to net income. It is the full cycle that sits behind any set of financial statements, done by hand once.
